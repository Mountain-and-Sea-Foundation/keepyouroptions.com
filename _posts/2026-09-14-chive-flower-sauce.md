Last week my chives went to flower, and instead of letting the blossoms drop, I cut a basket full and turned them into a small jar of chive flower sauce.

That's the other kind of patience this blog is named for. Not every post here is about calls and puts. Some of it is just the garden, and the waiting that comes with it. This particular condiment has its own long patience behind it too: it's a version of a dish that's been made in northern China for centuries, ground fresh chive flowers cured in salt, traditionally served alongside mutton hot pot.

## Chive Flower Sauce

**What you'll need**

- 1 cup fresh chive blossoms, gently washed and patted completely dry
- 1 to 2 teaspoons coarse sea salt or flaky salt

**How to make it**

Start by shaking the blossoms outside to knock loose any tiny bugs hiding in the petals, then rinse them gently and pat them completely dry with a paper towel. Any leftover moisture will spoil the mixture, so don't rush this part.

Snip off the tough green stems and pull the individual florets apart from each head.

Place the blossoms and salt into a small food processor, or work them by hand with a mortar and pestle if you'd rather.

Pulse just until the flowers break down forming a bright green paste. Stop before it turns into a runny liquid. This part goes faster than you'd expect if you use a food processor.

Transfer the paste to a clean glass jar and seal it. Keep it in the refrigerator; it's good to eat right away as a fresh condiment, and the flavor will deepen and mellow over the next several days as it cures.

![Finished chive flower sauce in a glass jar](/assets/images/2026/09/chive-flower-sauce/chive-flower-sauce-jar.jpg)

Use it the way it's traditionally served, as a dipping sauce alongside hot pot or grilled meat, or simply stirred into noodles or spooned over eggs. A cup of blossoms doesn't look like much once it's ground down, and that's the part worth sitting with. You wait for the flowers, you wait again while it cures, and what you get back is small and concentrated and worth more than the sum of what went in. Sounds familiar.
